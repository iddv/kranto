# The floor

Every post Kranto writes follows these rules. Each says what to do; "Not:" is the shape to avoid
and "Do:" a better one, both from real drafts. The tells come from Humanizer (MIT),
slop-forensics (MIT) and Wikipedia's *Signs of AI writing*; see NOTICE. The person's voice card
and samples win where they differ (if their samples use dashes, use dashes at about their rate).

## Make a claim
1. **Open on a claim, a prediction or a question the reader cares about,** then let the sources
   back it and tell the story. The angle gives it. Never announce the structure or count the
   points.
   Not: "Three things point in this direction. First, memory tooling is growing."
   Do: "Nobody checks whether shared agent memory leaks one user's secrets to another. A new
   preprint shows how you would."
2. **Trust the reader.** Name the source once, plainly, and move on: what is missing is implied.
   Give one caveat at most, and only when it changes the point.
   Not: "The sources give no details beyond this." / "So the honest reading is this: …" / "It
   is worth keeping in mind that this comes from the FT's reporting." / "self-reported" twice.
   Do: "The FT says regulators feared the robots' data would leave the country to train AI."
3. **Take a stance when the angle has one, and be willing to be wrong.** A prediction is good:
   people answer a prediction. This is one person's writing, not a company's.
   Do: "My bet: this is the first of many robotics deals stopped over data."
4. **Tell the reader why they should care, first.** Open on their interest, not on the news'
   subject and verb. A post that only restates facts has failed, even when every fact is right.
   Not: "A3 has published ISO 21423:2026, developed by ISO Technical Committee 299."
   Do: "Running robots from three vendors in one warehouse? A new ISO standard says they should
   all speak the same language."
5. **Let the angle choose the shape.** Sometimes three paragraphs, sometimes one and a question.
   No fixed sections, no sections of equal length, no "What happened / Why it matters" on
   every post.
   Not: "## What happened", "## Why it may matter", "## What we don't know yet", four sections
   of about 90 words.
   Do: the claim in two sentences, the one fact that carries it, the story behind it, and what
   you would watch for next.

## Say it directly
6. **State the claim directly.** Use a contrast only when the reader really holds the belief you
   correct, and both halves carry a fact. Not: "It's not just a tool, it's a shift." / "This
   isn't about speed. It's about trust."
7. **End on something with weight:** the last concrete fact, a prediction or a real question.
   Not: "Let that sink in." / "That's the real win." / "And that changes everything." / a
   last sentence that only says why it matters ("It shows how far that gets.").
8. **Open with the point itself.** Not: "Here's the thing:" / "Let's dive in." / "Honestly?" /
   "The result? …"
9. **Say the specific claim instead of a saying.** Not: "At its core, what really matters
   is…" / "Trust is the currency of the web."
10. **Answer only objections someone made.** Not: "I'm not saying X, but…" / "Don't get me
    wrong…" / "To be clear,…"
11. **Let the facts carry the weight; don't add a moral or a stock hook.** Not: "Ultimately,
    it's a reminder that…" / "The future looks bright." / "Only time will tell." / "Worth
    watching." / "It remains to be seen."

## Rhythm
12. **List as many items as there are.** Two is fine, four is fine; three only when there are
    three. Not: "fast, simple, and powerful" three times in one post.
13. **Join clauses with commas, colons, semicolons, "and" or "but", or brackets; keep related
    clauses in one sentence.** Not: an em dash (—) as the default joint in every other
    sentence, nor a string of clipped sentences in its place.
14. **Vary sentence and paragraph length, and how sentences open,** the way speech does: a
    short sentence after a long one, a one-line paragraph when it lands a point. Not: four
    paragraphs of three sentences each, nor "It… It… It…" three times in a row.
15. **Put a fact in its own sentence.** Not: a trailing "-ing" clause that adds unearned weight:
    "…, highlighting the growing importance of…" / "…, underscoring its role in…".
16. **Use is, are and has.** Not: "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features".

## Words
17. **Use the plain word.** Not: delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, robust (unless
    technical), seamless, leverage, landscape (unless land), realm, navigate (unless a route),
    foster, underscore, showcase, intricate, multifaceted, vibrant, bustling, nestled,
    game-changer, groundbreaking, revolutionize, unlock, unleash, elevate, empower, harness,
    cutting-edge, ever-evolving, journey (unless travel), insights, actionable, holistic,
    synergy, paradigm, transformative, moreover, furthermore, additionally.
18. **Be bold with claims, plain with words.** Not: "excited to share", "thrilled", "huge news",
    "a must-read", "mind-blowing".
19. **Name who said it.** Not: "experts say", "observers note", "industry reports suggest".
20. **Leave out chat leftovers.** Not: "I hope this helps", "Great question", "Let me know in
    the comments" (unless the person writes that way).

## Truth
21. **The stance is the angle's; the facts are the sources'.** Argue the angle as the person's
    own, in the first person when it reads better. Never invent an experience they did not
    write. Not: "I tried it last week." / "At my company we…" / "I've been bitten by this."
22. **Every fact comes from the sources,** with their numbers, names and quotes. Leave out what
    they don't give, without saying it is missing.
